This website is an Alternative Learning Assessment (ALA) for JR 213: Multimedia Journalism, created by third-year Bachelor of Arts in Journalism students at West Visayas State University (WVSU) Main Campus. Using a mix of text, audio, video, images, and interactive elements, it tells a compelling investigative story about the clash between farming, environmental damage, and flooding in the towns of Batad and Balasan in the Province of Iloilo, Phillippines, showcasing our ability to research, produce, and present high-quality journalistic stories.

Batad’s economy depends on yellow corn because its quick 100-day harvest provides fast, reliable income. Farmers cannot afford the years it takes to grow sustainable alternatives like fruit trees. However, expanding corn farms and heavy chemical use have destroyed 80% of Batad’s forests, causing severe soil erosion and watershed damage.

The neighboring Municipality of Balasan suffers from severe flooding. Many residents blamed Batad's deforestation and Alapasco Lake for the deadly 2019 Typhoon Ursula floods. However, disaster officials clarify that Balasan is a natural geographic catch basin. The 2019 disaster was actually caused by the collapse of Balasan's own earth dam and defective drainage system, not water spilling over from Batad.

Both towns are trying to adapt. Batad is promoting eco-tourism, enforcing forest protections, and relocating farmers from landslide zones. Balasan is clearing drainage systems and planting trees. However, the main challenge remains clear: finding a way to support the farmers' daily income without sacrificing the surrounding environment of both towns.

PAANO NAG-ANAW?: A flood of Progress or Devastation?

The Municipality of Batad is widely celebrated for its eastern shorelines that yield a steady coastal catch, while its sprawling flatlands and steeply rolling hills serve as the engine of an intensive inland agricultural boom. It is upon these very slopes that Batad forged its proudest identity as the "Corn Capital of Iloilo," a powerhouse driven by endless rows of yellow corn, rice, and sugarcane.

Yet, this title masks a complex socio-economic reality. Public discourse, driven by local crowdsourced accounts and investigative reports from platforms like the Daily Guardian, has brought to light a growing environmental crisis stemming from the uncontrolled expansion of commercial corn farming on the town's vulnerable public lands.

The Root Cause: In an article "YELLOW FLOOD’: Batad faces growing flood risks as corn expands and forests shrink" published by Daily Guardian, the town's Comprehensive Land Use Plan (CLUP) reveals that about 80% of Batad's forest cover has been lost over the past two decades. Data from the Municipal Agriculture Office in 2025 indicates that corn plantations have aggressively taken over forestland across eight barangays. Much of this denudation is concentrated in critical watershed areas like Barangay Alapasco, where hectares of corn farms now operate on land officially classified as protected forest.

Salvador Manglinong, Jr., officer-in-charge of Iloilo's Provincial Environment and Natural Resources Office (PENRO), explains that the core problem lies in the farming practices used for genetically modified (GMO) yellow corn. Plantations rely heavily on chemical weed killers, particularly glyphosate, which strip the land of its natural water-retention abilities and worsen soil erosion. Furthermore, Manglinong notes that these chemicals have crippled government reforestation efforts, such as the National Greening Program, because herbicide applications often kill planted tree seedlings alongside unwanted weeds.

Local residents and netizens are actively calling out these systemic failures. Netizen, Hector Teodosio stated, "Because of uncontrolled corn planting even on public lands, and the widespread use of weed killer chemicals, the top soil, where most soil nutrients are there, are all washed out making it difficult for plants to grow". Teodosio heavily criticized the lack of government oversight, describing these as "environmental criminal acts" allegedly permitted by local government officials in conspiracy with national agricultural and environmental agencies.

The Ecological Domino Effect Ricardo “Richie” Escanlar, a geologist from the Iloilo City government, warns that converting forests into corn farms amplifies flooding risks, as forested areas possess better water retention and root systems that provide slope stability. This is supported by studies from the farmer-scientist network MASIPAG and a 2020 study by Dr. Fernando Siringan, which explicitly link upland yellow corn cultivation to forest loss, severe erosion, and worsening downstream floods.

Melvin Purzuelo, executive director of Green Forum Panay Guimaras, Inc., adds that the resulting sedimentation reduces the storage capacity of dams, rivers, and creeks. The consequences of this overflow are highly visible to neighboring communities. Expressing dismay over the spillover effect, online commentator Ave Rex Andrada lamented, "Kaluluoy gid Ang Balasan, tanan nga tubig sa dam sang Batad, deretso gid tanan sa banwa sang Balasan" (Balasan is pitiful, all the water from Batad's dam goes straight to the town of Balasan).

The Human Cost The consequences of these compromised watersheds have been devastating for downstream residents. The town's CLUP identified rapid surface runoff from the bare mountains as a contributing factor to flash floods, including those during Typhoon Ursula in 2019 that killed eight people in the downstream barangay of Pasayan. Local crowdsourced accounts similarly link the severe disaster impacts and mortalities of Typhoon Ursula directly to these aggressive agricultural practices.

Today, communities continue to bear the brunt of this ecological shift. Residents like Rogema Diel of Barangay Bulak Sur report that they are regularly isolated by swelling streams during heavy rains, sometimes forcing families to wait weeks for water levels to recede enough for safe passage.

Balancing Batad’s socio-economic survival with the urgent need for forest protection, reforestation, and sustainable agroforestry remains critical to preventing future tragedies in the region's vulnerable downstream communities. https://www.dailyguardian.com.ph/blog/batad-faces-growing-flood-risks-as-corn-expands-and-forests-shrink

At a Tipping Point

A view of the Batad Municipal Hall along the Iloilo East Coast–Capiz Road in Poblacion, Batad, Iloilo, where the team interviewed representatives from the Municipal Planning and Development Office (MPDO), Municipal Agriculture Office (MAO), Municipal Environment and Natural Resources Office (MENRO), Municipal Disaster Risk Reduction and Management Office (MDRRMO), and Municipal Tourism Office about Alapasco's corn plantation.

Corn Capital at a Crossroads

Named after the batad-batad shell, the municipality of Batad is a coastal settlement in Iloilo Province with 24 distinct barangays and a population of 22,174 as of 2024. It is not to be mistaken with Batad, Ifugao.

It is where Alapasco Lake, famous for its sights and tourism, is located. Visitors visit the artificial reservoir built for agriculture and irrigation to take pictures, sight-see, camp, fish, and go boating.

Batad once was the Malay settlement called Dulangan, meaning “landing place”. The Spanish arrived and named it Embarcedero and made it a barrio of Balasan. It was officially recognized as a town on October 30, 1949.

The municipality has a flourishing industry of corn farming. Alongside fishery and general agriculture, Batad relies on corn as a major crop and source of livelihood. The land is abundant and fertile, having the ideal conditions for rice and corn production. Batad is dubbed the “Corn Capital of Iloilo” for these very reasons.

Recent accusations and speculations from bigger platforms such as Daily Guardian, point to this specific corn industry and the lack of environmental regulation surrounding these farmlands, as the reason behind multiple mortalities from Typhoon Ursula in 2019.

There is valid reasoning behind these allegations. However, more research reveals that there is an overall lack of flood control projects in Batad and surrounding areas.

Although the local government has offered intercropping and sustainable agriculture as alternatives to conventional farming specifically to replace expansive corn plantations, one local resident voiced the community’s quiet reluctance to invest in different crops.

In an exclusive interview with our team, Edita Chavez, a native of Alapasco, corn farmer, and owner of the booming Hilltop View Eat and Chill, explained why locals choose to stick to their accustomed farming practices.

Ever since rice fields were converted into a dam for the area’s water development, a project that displaced Chavez’s former home and other families, residents have relied on the corn industry because it only takes 100 to 120 days from planting to harvest.

Farmers have maintained this practice for years and have grown protective of their municipality's title as the corn capital of the province. They sustain their yields by sowing seeds and applying a mixture of Triple 14 and Urea fertilizers for rapid leaf and stalk growth. To them, this quick turnaround is much more practical than waiting several years for coconut and fruit-bearing trees to mature, especially given the struggle with pests, which can also crowd out other crops and reduce overall yields.

As much as farmers want to rejuvenate the soil by letting the land rest for a few years to achieve better future production, doing so would disrupt their daily income and livelihood.

For Chavez and many other families in Batad, shifting to sustainable alternatives is not simply a matter of changing seeds, it is a financial gamble they cannot yet afford to take. Until programs offer a way to bridge the income gap during transition periods, the municipality's rolling hills will likely remain golden with corn.

The local government of Batad has since shared many different programs and initiatives to counter the environmental repercussions that the corn farming industry has brought. The team sat down with Mr. John Evan Palmares from MAO, Mr. Gilbert de la Cruz from MDRRMO, Mr. Rudolf Charles Intelligando from the Planning Office, and Mr. Daniel de los Santos from the Tourism Office at the Batad Municipal Hall on November 12, 2026.

Their newer AIP or Annual Investment Programs observe focus on the agricultural and socio-economic sectors. The LGU has also partnered with NISO and civil society organizations to encourage agri-ecotourism, create ordinances focusing on the sustenance of farmers and preservation of timberland, launch tree planting, coastal clean-ups and more, and overall, achieve their goal of becoming “a premiere destination for agritourism.” To achieve this goal of becoming an ecotourism destination, the LGU wants the hills green again instead of bare and brown as they believe it will add to Alapasco’s appeal.

According to MAO, the corn is the backbone of the farmers’ livelihoods, and they have limited means of acquiring capital which is why they intercrop fruit trees and coconuts as they wait for the corn to mature. MENRO also stated that with fewer trees on the slopes, heavy rain washes topsoil into the Alapasco reservoir, silting up the dam and river. The Planning and Tourism Offices added that Alapasco is classified as timberland and a landslide risk, which is why the town discourages corn planting there through an ordinance. This risk is according to the Mines and Geosciences Bureau. The Planning Office said that the LGU hopes to gradually shift farmers into intercropping and towards root crops.

For farmers like Edita Chavez, none of this makes the choice simple. Some of the LGU’s initiatives like the Resilience Village in Nangka, Batad, built to rehome farmers from landslide-prone areas have been implemented by the Batad LGU. Many of these programs remain in planning as of the present and how well they work for farmers like Chavez remains to be seen.

Beyond the Truth

Resident of Brgy. Tingui-an, Balasan, Iloilo, led the team through their home, detailing the severe floodwater levels and hardships their family endured when Typhoon Ursula (Phanfone) struck on Christmas Day, December 25, 2019.

Beyond the Alapasco Myth: Uncovering the True Causes of Balasan’s Floods

Flooding is one of the worst enemies of the Municipality of Balasan that took away lives, destroyed livelihoods and instilled fear among residents. For many years, residents believed that the flooding tragedy they experience every time a typhoon hits is caused by the overtopping water from the Alapasco lake or dam, most especially Barangay Maya and Tingui-an where surging floodwater directly passes by down to neighboring barangay.

According to the residents of Balasan, they have experienced severe flooding ever since people from Batad started to replace trees over yellow corn plantations and used pesticides and herbicides that lead to soil erosion and low water retention.

In an interview with Joven Zabala, head of Balasan Municipal Environment and Natural Resources Office, he stated that it is truly discouraged to plant corn on the high slopes of the mountains because it is one of factors behind soil erosion due to there’s no longer trees to prevent soil degradation.

Upon acknowledging that Batad rising corn plantations may contribute to flooding, Zabala also emphasized that it is not the primary reason for flooding in their municipality. He added that during their quarterly MENRO meeting along with other municipalities they raised these issues and can assure that the MENRO of Batad have their own initiative and ongoing solution towards the recurring issue of flooding and deforestation.

In support to Zabala, Chinky Diolosa, head of Balasan Municipal Disaster Risk Reduction and Management Office emphasized that Balasan is a catch basin municipality and according to the Hazard Mapping of Balasan from the Department of Environment and Natural Resources- Mines and Geosciences Bureau or DENRMGB among 23 barangays of Balasan, there are 18 with flood plumes and only 5 barangays are not often affected.

She added that there's also no facts or data that can support the residents' claim that the Alapasco lake causes the flooding they often experience. According to her, the water from the Alapasco lake which residents believed passes through Brgy. Maya and Tingui-an have a different route and the real reason for massive flooding is the water that came from the mountains and the two owned Water Impounding Facilities of Balasan namely Zaragosa and Batuan Dam.

Diolosa debunks the residents claim that during the tropical cyclone Ursula where Balasan specifically Brgy. Maya and Tingui-an experienced an above 10 feet flood that reached the house's roof is caused by the overtopping water of the Alapasco lake but rather caused by the sudden collapse of their Batuan Water Impounding Project that is not a structured dam but rather an earth dam that basically could not hold a huge volume of water that loosen the soil and made the water from the upland rushed down to the low lying areas without the residents knowing it. She also pointed out that Balasan has a defective drainage system because of the sudden introduction of Gaisano.

In order to prevent the same tragedy from happening, Balasan MDRRMO conducted a barangay assembly for hazards faced by their municipality twice a year mandated by the Department of Interior and Local Government or DILG, they also have a group chat that includes the all Barangay Captain where they can feed all the information about disaster preparedness. They have funds allocated for preventive measures which 70% of the 5% MDRRM fund is for the prevention and mitigation.

Regularly they conducted declogging in every drainage and they also have initiative with the Local Government Unit or LGU such as tree planting activities not only for the mangrove forest but also includes upland forest.

Where The Hills Meet The Harvest

Behind the green ranges of mountains and the shimmering lights of the lake, hides the struggles and challenges that often go overlooked. Brgy. Alapasco in Batad is known for its lush green mountain ranges and a clear man-made lake that sits in between. On the way up to Alapasco Hilltop View Eat and Chill, the hills of Batad roll out in every direction. People call it the “New Zealand of Iloilo,” and it’s easy to see why. Down below the hiking trail, a man-made dam holds the water that keeps nearby farms alive. For many families here, that water means the difference between a good harvest and a hard year. The dam is the Alapasco Small River Impounding Project, or also known as Alapasco Lake to most people. It was built to catch rainwater and runoff for the farms, and it stretches nearly 265 meters long and 21 meters high. But the calm water and the green hills around it have drawn more and more visitors, and the lake has become Batad’s growing eco-tourism spot. Visitors often come for the view, yet leaving with the story that comes with it. On November 12, 2026, when our team took the photos, the harvest was over. The hills of Alapasco were clear and dry, with only dried up stems and bare soil left where green corn once stood. With the planting season running from August to October, and the farmers spending 100-110 before they can harvest, the now empty- looking hills are really the quiet after a season of hardwork. Batad is known as the corn capital of Iloilo, and for most families here, corn is how the bills get paid and the children get fed. Edita Chavez, owner of Alapasco Hilltop View Eat and Chill, has lived in this barangay all her life. She remembers when the family’s wide farmland was reshaped for watershed management, and she has watched the land change around her. Like many in Batad, she and her family still depend on corn. Behind every view of these hills is someone like Edita, who has stayed, worked the land, and kept going.
